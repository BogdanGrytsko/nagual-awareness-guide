# Research notes: Michael Bernard Beckwith, Ernest Holmes, "The Secret"

Saved from a Claude Code conversation, 2026-10-25. Angle: the roots of the movement, and how
each person built a self-employed spiritual/self-help business.

Reliability: items marked (verified) were checked by web search during the conversation.

## 1. Michael Bernard Beckwith

### Bio
- **Born:** July 21, 1956 (verified, Wikipedia). An earlier answer said Nov 8; that was wrong.
- **Raised:** Los Angeles, in a middle-class African American family with a mainstream Christian upbringing.
- **Education:** University of Southern California. He says he started out pre-med.
- **Drugs and arrest (verified, his own account):** He used and **sold marijuana** to pay
  for college. An informant tipped off police, who raided his last deal and arrested him. At trial his
  lawyer demanded the informant appear in court. The informant refused because he feared for his life,
  so the judge let Beckwith go. Beckwith presents his "spiritual awakening" as coming from this trial
  plus a lucid dream in his twenties.
  - My note: the awakening came at a very convenient moment, and the "conversion
    after hitting bottom" story is itself a classic marketing pattern in this genre.

### Career and business
- He trained and was ordained in **Religious Science** (Science of Mind), the system Ernest Holmes founded.
- **1986:** He founded the **Agape International Spiritual Center**. It started as a Religious Science
  church and later called itself "transdenominational." It was in Culver City for years and is now at
  8440 Wilshire Blvd, Beverly Hills (verified). Reported membership is in the thousands.
- He co-founded the **Association for Global New Thought**.
- He co-founded the **Season for Nonviolence** with Arun Gandhi (verified).
- **Books:** *Inspirations of the Heart*, *40 Day Mind Fast Soul Feast*, *A Manifesto of Peace*,
  *Spiritual Liberation* (2008, Nautilus Gold Medal 2009) and *Life Visioning* (2012) (verified).
- **PBS special:** *The Answer Is You* (2009) (verified). A pledge-drive special is a common
  distribution route for self-help authors.
- **Wife:** Rickie Byars (Beckwith), a singer-songwriter who led Agape's music. Music releases extended the brand.

### Growth playbook
1. A local church/community gave him a following and steady income (1986 onward).
2. Books and a branded method (**Life Visioning Process**) gave him a product.
3. **The Secret (2006)** took him from regional to global (see section 3).
4. Mass media followed: Oprah and Larry King (around 2007), then PBS (2009).
5. He sells courses, retreats, teacher/minister training, livestreams and conference talks.

**Conclusion:** *The Secret* was not the start of his career. It **scaled up a business he was
already running** (Agape had been operating for about 20 years). He already had a product and a
following, so when the break came he could turn the attention into money.

### Life Visioning Process
This is a moderate success, not a phenomenon. It works as a signature method for selling books,
courses and workshops (Oprah.com featured it in 2018, verified), but it depends on his name and does
not stand on its own.

### Legal record
No lawsuits or fraud allegations against Beckwith or Agape turned up in searches.
Wikipedia flags its own article on him as containing promotional content.

## 2. Ernest Holmes (1887–1960): the roots

### Bio (verified)
- **Born:** January 21, 1887, on a poor farm in Lincoln, Maine, the youngest of nine sons.
- **Age 15:** He moved to Boston, worked in retail, and studied public speaking at the
  Leland Powers School of Expression (1908–1910). There he read Mary Baker Eddy (Christian
  Science), Emerson, Troward, Larson, Quimby and others.
- **1912:** He moved to Venice, California, to join his brother **Fenwicke Holmes**.
- He was ordained a Divine Science minister before he started his own movement.
- He died April 7, 1960.

### Where the money came from (verified, mostly from Center-affiliated biographies)
He did **not** start with outside capital. He bootstrapped it, services first:
1. **He charged for "mental healing" treatments.** Early on he reportedly asked to be paid in
   **gold pieces**, and he "never hesitated to charge for his treatments."
2. **Paid classes.** Courses cost up to **$25 each** (a large sum in the 1910s–20s, roughly
   several hundred dollars today), with as many as **1,000 attendees**. That was his main early
   income. Free public lectures (for example at the Strand Theater, LA) served as the funnel into
   the paid classes.
3. **A family business with his brother.** Ernest and Fenwicke ran the *Southern California
   Metaphysical Institute* in LA and a **"mental healing sanitarium"** in Long Beach, which earned
   money from students and patients.
4. **Their own periodical early on.** In **1916** the brothers launched *Uplift*, a mental-healing
   magazine. It was small and cheap to produce, and it worked as both a marketing channel and a product.
5. **Books came out of the classes.** *Creative Mind* (1919) and *The Science of Mind*
   (1926) turned the course material into a product that could sell at scale. A printing run cost
   little compared with what the class income could cover.
6. **Wealthy LA backers and a nonprofit structure.** "Important people in Los Angeles" urged
   him to incorporate. In **February 1927** he formed the *Institute of Religious Science and School of
   Philosophy, Inc.* as a **nonprofit** with a Board of Governors. That gave him a vehicle for
   donations and tax-exempt operation. Its first office was on Wilshire Blvd.
7. ***Science of Mind* magazine (1927)** was published by the Institute. It was funded
   by the organization (subscriptions, donations, class revenue), not by him personally. It
   has been in print continuously since 1927.

The individual backers and dollar amounts were **not found** in searches. A full
biography would cover them, for example Fenwicke Holmes's *Ernest Holmes: His Life and Times* (1970).

### Growth playbook (verified)
- He began with small private meetings around LA. A 1916 talk at the Metaphysical Library led
  to repeat bookings and then a **national lecture tour**.
- He moved to bigger venues step by step: Ambassador Hotel theatre, **625** seats (1926), then Ebell
  Theatre, **1,295** seats (1927), then the Biltmore Hotel, then the Wiltern Theatre, **2,800+** seats.
- Celebrity followers included **Cecil B. DeMille, Peggy Lee, Cary Grant** (Wikipedia).
- He had **radio and TV** shows called *This Thing Called Life*.
- **A structure that could be copied, much like a franchise:** the Institute trained and licensed
  practitioners and ministers, who then opened their own churches under the brand. It was reorganized
  as the **Church of Religious Science** in 1954. Today it continues as **Centers for Spiritual
  Living**, the Affiliated New Thought Network and Global Religious Science Ministries.

### Was it a big success?
**Yes, over the long run.** *The Science of Mind* has been in print continuously since 1926
and has gone through many editions. It was heavily revised and expanded in 1938 with Maude Allison
Lathem, and later published by Dodd Mead and by Penguin (Tarcher). Sales total "hundreds of thousands"
across all editions. That is **not** a mass bestseller like *The Secret*, but it is a perpetual
seller with a **captive market**: every student in every Religious Science church buys it as the
required textbook. The magazine has run for nearly 100 years. Today's
CSL network has hundreds of centers.

### The link to Beckwith
Beckwith essentially **reran Holmes's playbook** in the same city (LA), with the same steps:
local congregation, then classes, then books, then his own media, then a training pipeline.
His biggest difference was the outside boost from *The Secret* and Oprah.

## 3. "The Secret" (2006)

- Rhonda Byrne (Australia) produced a film in 2006, followed by a book. Both were huge commercial
  successes: tens of millions of copies sold, with an Oprah boost in 2007.
- **How the teachers were picked (verified, Byrne's own account):** she flew from Australia to
  the US and followed one teacher's lead to the next. In about **7 weeks** her crew filmed **55
  teachers** (120 hours), and **24** made the final cut. Beckwith was one of them (credited "Rev.
  Dr. Michael Beckwith," "Visionary"). No source says who introduced him. As a prominent New Thought
  minister in LA he was an obvious person to include. No evidence that he paid to appear.
- Its roots are the same New Thought / "Law of Attraction" lineage as Holmes's work. Byrne has said
  Wallace Wattles's *The Science of Getting Rich* (1910) inspired her.

### Lawsuits and fraud (verified)
None of them amount to a Ponzi scheme or fraud by Byrne or Beckwith. What actually happened:
- **David Schirmer**, an Australian "wealth" teacher featured in the film:
  - In May 2007, *A Current Affair* (Australian TV) ran a segment called "The Secret Exposed"
    featuring people who said he owed them money.
  - Investors put close to **A$500k** into his **"Platinum Super Traders"** program and did not
    get it back (yes, that really was the name).
  - In **2010**, **ASIC banned him for life** from financial services. It found he had operated
    unlicensed, engaged in misleading/deceptive conduct, made false statements and acted dishonestly
    (Aug 2004 to Dec 2006).
  - He was also sued by Bob Proctor, another teacher from the film.
- **Rhonda Byrne**, civil disputes only:
  - Director **Drew Heriot** sued over promised profit share that was never paid (claim reported at
    up to $340M). Byrne countersued, and it apparently settled around 2009.
  - Internet marketer **Dan Hollings** made a similar broken-promise claim.
  - **Vanessa Bonnette** alleged 100+ instances of plagiarism from her 2003 book. It settled in 2007.
  - **Esther Hicks** was cut from later versions after Byrne refused to renegotiate her contract.
- **James Arthur Ray**, another teacher from the film: three people died at his 2009 "sweat lodge"
  retreat, and he was convicted of negligent homicide (from memory, not re-verified).

## Points for the essay
- **Same business model across 100 years:** free lecture, then paid class, then book, then the
  organization's own media, then certification/licensing of teachers (franchise), then celebrities
  for social proof, then a nonprofit/church structure (donations, tax exemption).
- **Holmes bootstrapped** with service income (healing treatments, $25 classes), then used the
  nonprofit structure to bring in wealthy LA backers.
- **Beckwith inherited the model** and the market. *The Secret* multiplied what he had, but he
  already had the infrastructure to turn the attention into money.
- **The "awakening" origin story** (Beckwith's arrest and trial) works as personal-brand mythology.
- **The weak point of the genre:** "wealth" teachers who run real money schemes (Schirmer) and
  intensive paid retreats that turn dangerous (Ray).

## Addendum: Agape's legal and tax structure (verified, 2026-10-25)

- **Legal entity:** *Agape International Spiritual Center of Truth Inc*, EIN **95-4804871**,
  Beverly Hills, CA. **501(c)(3)**, classified as a **Church**, an independent organization (not
  part of the Religious Science / CSL network). Contact: Michael Beckwith. Donations are tax-deductible.
- **Does not have to file Form 990.** US churches are exempt from filing it, so revenue, assets,
  board members and Beckwith's salary are **not public**. The "$0" in databases means "not
  reported," not that Agape has no income.
- **Comparison with Holmes:** Holmes used a nonprofit Institute with a board (1927) and later a
  Church (1954). Beckwith's setup is the same idea, but as a church it has even less
  disclosure than a regular charity.
- **Building:** the Culver City building flooded, was not up to code, and the landlord wanted to
  double the rent. In **July 2018** Agape moved into the rented **Saban Theatre**, Beverly Hills
  (about 1,900 seats vs about 1,200 before). The opening service featured Stevie Wonder (celebrity social proof).
- **Why the church structure helps, legally:** (a) the church pays no tax on donations and
  ministry income; (b) donors deduct their gifts; (c) no public financial filings; (d) under US
  law, ordained ministers can receive a tax-free **housing allowance** (IRC section 107) (from memory;
  whether Beckwith receives one is unknown). His salary is taxable personal income.
- **Unknown:** whether his book, course and speaking income goes to him personally, to a separate
  company, or to the church. Nothing public was found.

## Addendum: Agape demographics and target audience (2026-10-25)

No independent survey or census of Agape's congregation exists. Everything below comes from
Agape's own claims, journalism, or inference.

- **Size (self-reported):** Agape claims 10,000+ local members and "1 million unique hits annually"
  online across 212 countries. Wikipedia says 9,000 members. Services hold about 1,500–1,900
  (Saban Theatre). Agape also lists affiliate centers in Norfolk VA, Oakland and Irvine CA.
- **Diversity (as reported):** described as racially, culturally and religiously mixed: "Muslims, Jews,
  Christians, Buddhists, young, old, rich, and poor." A description that has been widely repeated (a Beliefnet
  2001-era piece, which I could not open to check) says services draw people dressed "from dashiki to Armani to aloha."
- **"40%+ Jewish":** this claim circulates in search snippets ("by some estimates"), and many
  reportedly stay active in their synagogues. The original source could not be opened, so treat it as **unverified**.
- **Celebrities associated with Agape (reported):** Stevie Wonder, Oprah Winfrey and Stedman Graham
  (Agape's own Facebook post welcomed them), Hilary Swank, Sterling K. Brown, Scott Bakula,
  Christina Applegate, Van Morrison, Ben Vereen, Deepak Chopra. These are "attended, endorsed or
  befriended," which is not the same as confirmed members.

### Target audience (my inference)
1. **"Spiritual but not religious" Angelenos.** People raised Christian or Jewish who left traditional
   religion but want community, music and ritual. Agape does not ask them to give up their original faith.
2. **Hollywood and creative industries.** The location (Culver City, then Beverly Hills), celebrity
   presence and a message of "manifesting your vision" suit people working in uncertain, career-driven fields.
3. **Educated middle and upper-middle class seekers.** These are people who buy the courses, practitioner
   training and retreats, and who read Oprah and *The Secret*.
4. **African American churchgoers who want a less dogmatic style.** Beckwith comes from a Black church
   background, and the gospel-influenced music (Rickie Byars) gives a familiar worship feel.
5. **Global online audience.** Livestream viewers who know him from *The Secret* and Oprah.

### Revenue model (from Agape's own site)
- Offering collections at services, **tithes**, gifts and **legacy giving** (bequests in wills).
- **Agape University**: paid courses, **Licensed Spiritual Practitioner** training, a ministerial program.
  This is the same certification pipeline Holmes used.
- **Practitioners in private practice charge a fee** for prayer "treatments." That is
  Holmes's 1910s paid-healing model, still running.
- Membership is free, with a free "New Members" course. That's the funnel: free entry, then paid
  classes, then certification, then a role in the organization.

## 4. Video analysis: *Super Soul Sunday* S7E15, "Living with Intention" (2026-10-26)

- **Source:** https://youtu.be/e9ocPP_eiuU (OWN, 42 min, Oprah interviews Beckwith)
- **Transcript:** `E:\TNZPromotion\yt_e9ocPP\transcript.txt` (YouTube auto-captions: "Fanny yamer" =
  Fannie Lou Hamer, "morous college" = Morehouse College)
- Planned use: the user's first essay, on **falsifiability**.

### Main points
1. **Ask empowering questions.** ("What's trying to emerge? What's my gift?") The universe answers through
   hunches, signs and dreams. Disempowering questions ("why me, who's to blame") also get answers, but as excuses.
2. **Everyone is a unique expression of God; life keeps unfolding.** You're never finished. "Grow where
   you're planted"; baby steps turn inertia into momentum.
3. **God always answers.** If nothing arrives, you weren't receiving or weren't "vibrationally ready."
4. **Let go.** Wanting hard sends "I don't have it" and blocks it. Release it like a coin in a wishing well
   (Oprah's tomato soup story).
5. **Four stages:** Victim (ends at "sick and tired of being sick and tired" + "help" = "Hello Eternal
   Loving Presence"), then Manifester (intentions, visualization, write it down, talk vision more than
   problems, "what if" questions, share only with selected friends, "talk to it, then from it"), then
   Channel (surrender, flow states), then Oneness ("my life is the life of God").
6. **Dark night of the soul** = the gap between the old identity and the new one. To get through it, ask:
   "If this experience were to last forever, what quality would have to emerge for me to have peace of mind?"
7. **"Pain pushes until the vision pulls."** "Potential is always bigger than the problem."
8. **Vibration.** "Everything is vibration." "You cannot have what you're not willing to become
   vibrationally; if you do get it you'll lose it" (his example: lottery winners). "You're not attracting,
   you're radiating."
9. **Like yourself when you're by yourself**, or you'll lean on others to make you happy.
10. **Reaction vs. choice.** More awareness lets you choose (Dalai Lama, Dr. King).
11. Biographical: he left religion young and was agnostic in college (Morehouse, then USC). Lucid dream,
    a year long, ending with three men, one stabbing him in the heart. He woke up seeing "light everywhere"
    and called it born again. (Compare section 1: his other account ties the awakening to the drug trial.)
12. Visioning vs. visualization: visualization (stage 2) means seeing what you want. Visioning means opening
    to "something within me I can't see," beyond parental or social fantasy.

### Practicality: 40/100
- **Psychological layer, about 70:** drop the victim story, ask "what now," take small steps, plan more than
  you complain, accept instead of resisting. These match CBT, ACT and locus-of-control research.
- **Metaphysical layer, about 5:** vibration, the universe answering, radiating. Removing it changes no
  practical instruction, so it adds claims without adding actions.
- **Some of it lowers the score:** visualizing emotionally "until it manifests" goes against the research
  (Oettingen), and the vibration frame blames people for their own bad circumstances.

### Falsifiability tally (about 25 distinct claims)
| Category | ~Count | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Unfalsifiable | 13 | universe answers sincere questions; God never errs; pain pushes / vision pulls; potential > problem; animosity slows vibration; radiating; "not evolving on plan" |
| Falsifiable and broadly supported | 8 | victim narrative keeps you stuck; co-rumination spirals down; small steps make momentum; self-acceptance; meditation lets you choose instead of react |

**Pattern:** every testable claim is psychological. The metaphysical claims either can't be tested, or
they can and fail.

### Self-sealing logic (the core for a Popper essay)
- You get it: manifestation worked. You don't get it: you weren't receiving, weren't sincere, or wanted
  it too much. You get it and lose it: you weren't vibrationally aligned. Things get worse: pain is
  pushing you toward a vision. "God is always answering" means there's no possible observation of no answer.
- **"Let go" rule:** wanting explains why it didn't come, and releasing explains why it did. Both outcomes
  fit, so it predicts nothing.
- **Equivocation:** physics "vibration" (fields, measurable) vs. emotional "frequency" (no unit). Same word,
  different meanings.
- **Truth by feeling:** Oprah's "I could cry," "this will preach," "that's going to resonate." On the show,
  resonance stands in for evidence.
- **Counterargument to address:** "It's practice, not science; judge it by its fruits" (William James,
  pragmatism). That position is fair, but taking it means giving up the "law" language ("that's the way
  it operates").
